How the model works

A one-employee shop sells custom, three-dimensional sheet-metal construction products (formed facade panels, sculpted canopies, curved louvers). Each order is a new design. Every design is built in a batch of n units. Q is units sold per year and D = Q / n is designs won per year.

TC(Q) = Fixed + Acquisition(L) + Labor(H) + D·c_design + Q·c_unit w = w₀ · 0.5^(lead weeks / half-life) win rate falls with lead time L = D / w qualified leads needed Acquisition(L) = c_L · [L + Lₛ/(γ+1) · (L/Lₛ)^(γ+1)] marginal lead cost c_L·(1 + (L/Lₛ)^γ) H = L·h_quote + D·(h_design + h_coord) + Q·h_unit Labor(H) = salary + max(0, H − capacity) · (salary / capacity) · premium AC = TC / Q MC = dTC/dQ MES = argmin AC ⇔ MC = AC

Why the AC curve turns up

With only fixed and constant per-unit costs, average cost falls forever and MC never meets AC. Two real constraints of a small custom shop bend it back up. Labor: the single employee runs out of hours, and overflow work costs a contractor premium. Local demand: custom construction work comes from a thin regional market, so each extra lead costs more than the last. Slow lead times make both worse. A lower win rate means more quotes, more unpaid customer hours and more marketing per order.

Reading the results

  • Break-even is the minimum viable scale: the smallest yearly volume where price covers average cost. It answers “how small can the business be?”
  • MES (MC = AC) is the bottom of the AC curve. With default assumptions it sits where the employee’s hours run out. For a one-person shop, the person sets the MES, not the machine.
  • Cost penalty at ½ MES is the classic industrial-organization measure of how much it hurts to be half the efficient size.
  • Die route: per-design costs are the die, its engineering, tryout, expected rework after design changes, storage, inspection, press setup and the financing of the die until the customer pays. Machina route: per-design costs are programming/first article, its financing and evaluation trips.

Where the synthetic numbers come from

  • Die cost: mid-complexity progressive dies commonly quote $15k–50k; complex multi-stage dies run $25k–150k+ (Jennison, ML Hardware).
  • Hidden die NRE: design engineering $3–5k, tryout $2–5k, storage $0–2k/yr (DRA Metal).
  • Machina: dieless forming moves lead time from months to days and removes die cost per design (Machina Labs). Machina does not publish part pricing. Cell rate, robot hours and programming fees here are assumptions to argue with.
  • Incremental forming trades tooling cost for cycle time: cheaper for small batches, slower per part (RoboDK).

Limits

Large facade panels are more often drawn in line or transfer dies than in progressive dies. The die cost slider covers either. The model ignores installation, taxes and capacity limits at Machina, and prices are held fixed rather than competed down. The profit-maximizing volume assumes overflow contractors are available without limit at the premium and that price holds. In practice, a shop with this cost advantage would cut price or see competitors adopt the same process, pulling price toward the dieless average cost.
